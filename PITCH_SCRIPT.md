### Slide 2. The problem (about 50 seconds)

The problem statement names five slow pressures: academic pressure, poor sleep, isolation, exhaustion, and loss of motivation.

The hard part is this: **each day looks fine.** So students only notice when their studies, friends or health already suffer.

**No single night looks bad. The drift does.**

*[Pause for one second.]*

🪝 This morning we spoke to ___ students here at the venue. One of them told us: "___". *(Use your best quote from the interviews.)*

So our question was: how can technology notice this early, and help, without diagnosing?

### Slide 3. Our solution (about 30 seconds)

MindMirror has two parts.

**The app.** A 30-second check-in every day. It compares you with your own normal and shows the proof. It gives you a "mind weather", a next step, and calm tools: games, breathing, and a companion to talk to.

**The AI necklace.** You hold the button, say how you feel, and hear a calm reply from the necklace itself. That talk shows up in the app. It's for the moments when you won't open an app.

Four ideas run through everything: compare you with yourself. Show the proof. Never diagnose. Point to real people.

### Slide 4. How it works (about 40 seconds)

Here is how it works, in five steps.

*[Move your hand left to right across the slide as you go.]*

One. You **check in**: mood, stress, energy, hours of sleep, and "did you talk to someone you like today?" You can tap, or just speak.

Two. MindMirror learns **your own normal** from your last two weeks. It waits at least five days before it says anything.

Three. It compares your last three days with your normal, and gives **five signals**. Each one says steady, watch, or needs care, with the proof.

Four. The signals make a **mind weather**: clear skies, passing clouds, soft rain, or heavy skies.

Five. The weather suggests a step on the **support ladder**: look after yourself, one small step, talk to a friend, or talk to someone trained.

🪝 One rule matters most: **the code does the maths, the AI only explains it.** The numbers come from plain, tested code. The AI only says them kindly. So it can't make things up.

### Slide 5. Five signals (about 30 seconds)

Each pressure in the problem statement has its own signal.

Academic pressure is **Pressure**. Poor sleep is **Sleep**. Isolation is **Connection**. Exhaustion is **Energy**. Loss of motivation is **Drive**.

*[Point at the card on the right.]*

This is what the student sees. "Sleep: needs care. You slept 5.4 hours on average the last 3 nights. Your usual is 7." And one small tip. Never a lecture.

### Slide 6. The AI necklace (about 40 seconds)

*[If the necklace is in the room, hold it up now. Judges love to see real hardware.]*

🪝 This is our AI necklace. We built it ourselves, from separate parts.

Inside, there is a small computer with Wi-Fi, a microphone, a speaker, and one button.

You hold the button and talk. Your voice goes over Wi-Fi to a small program on our laptop. It turns your voice into text, writes a short, kind reply, and turns that reply into a voice. The necklace speaks it back. And the conversation appears in the app, live.

It takes about ___ seconds. *(Fill in the time Pankaj measures.)*

One safety rule: if someone says crisis words, the AI is skipped. The necklace gives the helpline numbers, Tele-MANAS 14416 and 112, every time.

### Slide 7. Play room (about 30 seconds)

When you're stressed, reading tips doesn't help much. Doing something with your hands does.

So we built a **Play room**. No scores. No timers. No way to lose.

**Bubble wrap**: pop slowly, breathe out with each one. **Worry shredder**: type what's worrying you, and watch it shred and fade. Nothing is saved. **Ripples pond**: tap the water and watch calm rings spread.

🪝 *[Smile.]* I built these three games myself.

There is also a one-minute breathing circle, built by Kishore. So when a student sees "needs care", they get something to do in the next ten seconds.

### Slide 8. Safety (about 40 seconds)

Mental health needs trust. So safety is built in from the start.

**One. We never diagnose.** No labels. We only show changes against your own normal.

**Two. The code calculates, the AI explains.** The AI only gets the facts, says two short sentences, and never gives medical advice.

**Three. Your data stays with you.** Your check-ins stay in your own browser. No accounts. No server database. Your voice is used to understand you, and we don't store it.

🪝 **Four. Crisis goes to people.** If someone says something like "I want to die", the AI is skipped completely. The app and the necklace show Tele-MANAS 14416 and 112. Every single time.

## 3. What the app does, in simple words

**A day with MindMirror (use this if a judge asks "so what does it actually do?"):**

> In the morning, Asha opens MindMirror and answers five quick questions. It takes 30 seconds. The app compares her week with her own usual. It tells her: "Sleep needs care. You slept 1.6 hours less than usual." It suggests one small tip. Later, walking back from class, she presses the necklace and says "I'm so tired, I have two deadlines." It answers kindly. At night she pops some bubbles in the Play room. If things get heavy, the app gently points her to a friend, a counsellor, or Tele-MANAS.

## 4. How we solve the problem

The problem statement asks for technology that helps students **notice early signs, understand their patterns, and find support, without diagnosing.** We answer each part:

| The problem asks | We answer with |
|---|---|
| **Notice early signs** | A 30-second daily check-in, or the necklace. We catch the slow drift, not just bad days. |
| **Understand their patterns** | We compare you with **your own normal** and show **the proof** for every signal. |
| **Find support** | The mind weather picks a step on the support ladder. Calm tools help right away. Real helplines are one tap away. |
| **Without diagnosing** | We never use medical labels. Code does the maths, AI only explains. Crisis words skip the AI. |

**Why our way is different (say this if asked "what's new?"):**
- Other apps compare you with **everyone**. We compare you with **yourself**.
- Other apps say "you seem stressed". We show **the proof**: "1.6 hours less sleep than your usual."
- Meditation apps help **after** you know you're stressed. We help you **notice** it.
- A necklace you just **talk to**, for moments you won't open an app.

## 5. Tech stack in simple words

Say the simple version. If a judge wants the real names, they're in the right column (and Pankaj can explain more).

| Part | Simple words | Real names (for Q&A) |
|---|---|---|
| **The app** | A website that works on any phone or laptop. No install. | React, TypeScript, Vite, Tailwind CSS |
| **Animations and charts** | Makes things move gently and draws the graphs | framer-motion, Recharts |
| **Saving data** | Saves everything inside your own browser, not on our server | IndexedDB, through Dexie |
| **Signal engine** | Plain code that does the maths and is tested | TypeScript with unit tests |
| **The hub** | A small program on our laptop that the necklace talks to | Node.js |
| **Speech to text** | Turns your voice into words | Whisper (large-v3-turbo) on Groq |
| **The reply** | Writes a short, kind answer | GPT-OSS 20B, an open AI model, on Groq |
| **Text to speech** | Turns the answer into an Indian-English voice | Microsoft Edge neural voice (en-IN) |
| **Live updates** | Pushes each necklace talk into the app as it happens | Server-Sent Events |
| **Necklace brain** | A tiny computer with Wi-Fi | ESP32-WROOM-32 board |
| **Necklace ears and mouth** | A digital microphone, an amplifier and a small speaker | INMP441 mic, MAX98357A amp, 8 Ω speaker |
| **Necklace code** | We wrote it ourselves. It listens and speaks at once and finds the laptop by itself | Arduino C++, 2 × I2S audio, UDP auto-discovery |

**The whole system in one breath:**
> "The necklace sends your voice over Wi-Fi to our laptop. The laptop turns it into text, writes a kind reply, makes a voice, and sends it back. The app shows the conversation live, and keeps all your data in your own browser."

## 6. Hook points: how to keep the judges with you

Judges hear many pitches in one morning. These moments make them look up and remember yours.

**The big hooks (already in the speech):**
1. **The opening line.** "Burnout doesn't arrive in one day." Say it slowly, before you say your name. A strong first line beats "Good morning, we are…".
2. **Asha's story.** A person is easier to remember than a feature. Say "Asha" often.
3. **"No single night looks bad. The drift does."** This is your best line. Pause after it.
4. **A real student's quote** from the morning interviews. It shows you listened to real people.
5. **"The code does the maths, the AI only explains."** Judges worry about AI making things up. This line answers that before they ask.
6. **Holding up the necklace.** Real hardware in your hand wakes everyone up.
7. **The live necklace reply.** The room goes quiet while it thinks. Let it. Then smile.
8. **"I built these three games myself."** Personal and proud.
9. **"Crisis goes to people. Every single time."** Shows you take safety seriously.
10. **"One link for a whole college."** Shows it can really be used.
11. **Ending with the first line again.** It closes the circle and people remember it.

**Small tricks that work:**
- **Ask a question, then answer it.** "So how can a computer notice something you can't? By comparing you with yourself."
- **Use numbers they can feel.** "1.6 hours less sleep" is better than "a drop in sleep".
- **The rule of three.** "Notice early. Understand yourself. Reach out." Three things are easy to remember.
- **The pause.** Two seconds of silence after an important line feels long to you, but to the judges it feels confident.
- **Invite them in.** At the end, or if a judge visits the table: "Would you like to pop some bubbles?" or "Would you like to talk to the necklace?"

**Words to use** (they are the heart of MindMirror): *your own normal*, *the proof*, *we never diagnose*, *one small step*, *real people*.

**Words to avoid:** depressed, disorder, symptom, patient, diagnosis, "detects mental illness", "cures", "100% accurate". Say "changes", "your usual", "needs care".

## 8. How to speak: voice, body, eyes

**Voice**
- **Go slower than feels right.** Nerves make everyone fast. If it feels a little too slow to you, it's perfect for them.
- **Loud enough for the back of the room.** Speak to the person farthest away.
- **Pause** after each slide title and after every 🪝 line.
- **Smile while you speak.** It changes the sound of your voice, and it's a calm app.
- **Breathe out before you start.** One slow breath, then the first line.

**Body**
- Stand with both feet flat, shoulder-width apart. Don't sway.
- Hands: open and relaxed, at waist height. Use them to point at the slide or at teammates. Don't hold a pen to click.
- Face the judges, not the screen. Glance at the slide, then turn back to talk.
- Move only when you change topic (for example, walk to the laptop for the demo).

**Eyes**
- Look at one judge for one full sentence, then the next judge for the next sentence.
- Never read from the slide. The slide is for them, not for you.
- If you forget a line, look at the slide title. It will remind you. Say the main idea in any words and move on. Nobody else knows the script.

**Teammates (they don't speak, but they are seen)**
- Pankaj and Kishore stand to the side, facing the judges, looking interested. No phones.
- When you name them, they smile and nod.
- During the demo: Kishore hands you the necklace; Pankaj keeps the hub window on screen and quietly fixes things if needed.

## 9. Questions from judges

The full list (about 70) is in [JUDGE_QA.md](JUDGE_QA.md). These are the ones most likely to come first. Short and simple answers.

**How to answer any question:**
1. Say the answer first, in one sentence. Then one reason. Then stop.
2. "Thank you, good question" gives you two seconds to think. Use it.
3. If you don't know: "I don't know the exact number. We would find out by ___." Never make up a number.
4. If it's very technical, give the simple one-line answer, then: "Pankaj built that part. Can he add the details?" That's allowed and it shows teamwork.

**The likely questions:**

- **Isn't this just a mood tracker?**
  > A mood tracker shows what you typed. MindMirror compares you with your own normal across five signals and tells you what *changed*, with the proof, and what to do next.

- **Is this diagnosing?**
  > No. We never name a condition. We only show changes against your own normal and suggest small steps, or people to talk to.

- **What if someone says they want to end their life?**
  > The AI is skipped. The app shows the urgent help card with Tele-MANAS 14416 and 112, and the necklace says the same out loud. It's fixed in code, so it's the same every time. *(Offer to show it.)*

- **Where is the data stored?**
  > In the student's own browser. No accounts, no server database. Only the voice goes to the AI service, to be understood, and we don't keep it.

- **Where is AI used?**
  > Only in three places: turning voice into text, writing a short kind reply, and making the voice. The numbers and signals come from normal tested code, not AI.

- **Why a necklace? Why not just the phone?**
  > The app has the same Hold to talk button. The necklace is for moments you won't unlock your phone, like walking back from class. One press and you're heard, with no screen pulling you in.

- **Where do your thresholds come from?**
  > They're starting points, not medical claims. For sleep, 45 minutes below your usual is "watch" and 1.5 hours is "needs care". We'd tune them with a counsellor. The method, comparing you with yourself, is what matters.

- **Students can lie in check-ins.**
  > They'd only be lying to themselves, because nobody else sees it. And we look at changes over days, not one answer.

- **The demo uses sample data. Does it really work?**
  > Yes, it's the same code for real and sample data. We use sample data because a real normal takes two weeks to learn. *(Then do a quick live check-in.)*

- **What did you build during the hackathon?**
  > All of it: the app, the hub, the necklace code and the case. The idea and the plan were made before. Every library and AI tool we used is listed in our project page.

- **Did you use AI to write code?**
  > Yes, and we disclosed it. We used Claude and ChatGPT as helpers. We decided what to build, checked and tested the code, and each of us can explain our part.

- **How is it different from Wysa, Headspace or Calm?**
  > Those help after you know you're stressed. We help you notice it early, in your own numbers, with the proof, and then point you to a step or a person.

- **How would it reach students? How would you make money?**
  > Free for students. A college shares one link at orientation. Later, colleges could pay for a campus version with their own counsellor contacts. We'd never show anyone's personal data.

- **What did your interviews tell you?**
  > We spoke to ___ students. ___ of them said ___. One said: "___". *(Fill in on Sunday.)*

- **What did you learn?**
  > How to turn a real problem into something people can actually use, and how important it is to say things simply and safely when the topic is mental health.
